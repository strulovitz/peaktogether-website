\section*{Computational Linear Algebra}

We need to solve large problems quickly and accurately. Linear algebra is often the key to
computations in engineering and science and management and finance. This page aims to
provide an update on hard power and soft power---computers and math.

\bigskip

The 500 fastest computers in the world are listed on \textbf{top500.org/news}. The leaders are
mostly paid for and controlled by governments, and we can give their locations: Kobe,
Oak Ridge, Livermore, Wuxi, Guangzhou. The next group includes Italy, Switzerland,
Germany, France, Korea, Australia, Taiwan, UAE, UK, Russia, Spain, Saudi Arabia,
Finland, Norway, and Brazil. The scientific interest is in their speed and special processors.
A good source of information is \textbf{top500.org/resources/frequently-asked-questions}.

\bigskip

The benchmark tests are linear algebra problems---factoring a big matrix into $L$ times
$U$ (lower triangular times upper triangular) and then solving a system of linear equations
$Ax = b$. The actual test is in the High Performance LINPACK benchmark. Those prob-
lems are at the heart of Chapters 2 and 3, now speeded up by parallel processing.

\bigskip

The top machine achieves $415$ petaflops $= 415$ times $10^{15}$ double precision floating
point operations per second. This is with extremely careful coding for special hardware.
The important point is that ordinary computers have also seen a tremendous increase in
speed. And there are comparable savings from better algorithms.

\bigskip

\section*{Numerical Linear Algebra}

This is the subject of major research: \emph{fast algorithms for matrix computations}. The first
was thousands of years ago with ``elimination''. For that idea and any new one, part of
the test is to count the steps: in this case $n^3/3$ for $n$ linear equations with $n$ unknowns.
To compute eigenvalues and now singular values, big progress brought that also to $cn^3$.
Favorite textbooks among many good ones are

\begin{itemize}
    \item L. N. Trefethen and David Bau, \emph{Numerical Linear Algebra}, SIAM (1997)
    \item Gene Golub and Charles van Loan, \emph{Matrix Computations}, Johns Hopkins (2013).
\end{itemize}

For the mathematics of linear algebra (not focused on computation) we mention

\begin{itemize}
    \item Roger Horn and Charles Johnson, \emph{Matrix Analysis}, Cambridge (2013)
    \item R. Bhatia, \emph{Matrix Analysis}, Springer and \emph{Positive Definite Matrices}, Princeton
\end{itemize}

\bigskip

\section*{Randomized Numerical Linear Algebra}

The usual algorithms meet a barrier when the problem becomes very large. We can hardly
store and read all the data. Even matrix multiplication $AB$ becomes impossible. Eigenval-
ues hit their own barrier at matrix size $n = 10^4$ or $10^5$.

\bigskip

A solution has been found if the matrix has enough structure. Random samples
tell us about the matrix! This is a deep and beautiful subject---see a masterpiece by
P. G. Martinsson and J. A. Tropp in \emph{Acta Numerica} (2020) and arXiv: 2002.01387.
