\section*{Preface}

This is a linear algebra textbook with a new start. Chapter 1 begins as usual with vectors.
We see their linear combinations and dot products. Then the new ideas come with matrices.
Let me illustrate those ideas right away by an example.

\bigskip

Suppose we are given a 3 by 3 matrix $A$ with columns $a_1, a_2, a_3$:

$$
A =
\begin{bmatrix}
a_1 & a_2 & a_3
\end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
1 & 2 & 3 \\
3 & 4 & 7 \\
4 & 2 & 6
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Those columns are three-dimensional vectors. The first vectors $a_1$ and $a_2$ connect the
center point $(0,0,0)$ to the points $(1,3,4)$ and $(2,4,2)$. The picture shows those points in
3-dimensional space ($xyz$ space). The key to this matrix is the third vector $a_3$ going to the
point $(3,7,6)$.

\bigskip

When I look at those vectors, I see something exceptional. Adding columns 1 and 2
produces column 3. In other words $a_1 + a_2 = a_3$. In a 3-dimensional picture, $a_1$ and $a_2$
go from the center point $(0,0,0)$ to the points $(1,3,4)$ and $(2,4,2)$. The picture shows how
to add those vectors. It is normal that all combinations of two vectors will fill up a plane.
(The plane is actually infinite, we just drew the part between the vectors.) What is
really exceptional is that \textbf{the third point $a_3 = (3,7,6)$ lies on this plane of $a_1$ and $a_2$.}

\bigskip

Most points don't lie on that plane. Most vectors $a_3$ are \emph{not} combinations of $a_1$ and
$a_2$. Most 3 by 3 matrices have \emph{independent} columns. Then the matrix will be invertible.
But these three columns are \emph{dependent} because they lie on the same plane: $a_1 + a_2 = a_3$.

\bigskip

\noindent vi

\bigskip

That picture reveals the most important fact about those three vectors $a_1, a_2, a_3$.
But we need the right language to describe it. Our goal is to see the idea, and to get better
and better at expressing it. Here are three steps in a good direction.

\bigskip

\noindent\textbf{1 \quad Idea in words} \hfill Column 3 is the sum of column 1 and column 2

\bigskip

\noindent\textbf{2 \quad Idea in symbols} \hfill $a_3 = a_1 + a_2$

\bigskip

\noindent\textbf{3 \quad Matrix times vector}
$$
\begin{bmatrix} a_3 \end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix} a_1 & a_2 \end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}
$$

\bigskip

Step 3 shows how a matrix $C$ multiplies a vector $x$. The columns $a_1$ and $a_2$ in $C$ multiply
the numbers $x_1 = 1$ and $x_2 = 1$ in $x$. \emph{The output $Cx$ is a combination of the columns.}
Here that column combination is $a_1 + a_2$.

One more crucial step allows several combinations at once. This is the way forward.
We cannot take this step with pictures or words. \textbf{A matrix multiplies a matrix.}

\bigskip

\noindent\textbf{4 \quad Matrix times matrix} \quad $A = CR$ is
$$
\begin{bmatrix} a_1 & a_2 & a_3 \end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix} a_1 & a_2 \end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}
$$

\bigskip

Those three columns of $R$ give three combinations in $A$ of the two columns in $C$:

\medskip

\noindent \textbf{Column 1:} $\; 1a_1 + 0a_2 = a_1$ \hfill \textbf{Column 2:} $\; 0a_1 + 1a_2 = a_2$

\noindent \textbf{Column 3:} $\; 1a_1 + 1a_2 = (1,3,4) + (2,4,2) = (3,7,6) = a_3$

\bigskip

That matrix multiplication $A = CR$ displays major information:

\begin{itemize}
    \item $A$ has dependent columns: the combination of columns $1 + 2 - 3$ gives $(0,0,0)$
    \item $A$ also has dependent rows: some combination of its rows will give $(0,0,0)$!
    \item The ``column space'' of this $A$ is only a plane and not the whole 3D space
    \item The ``row space'' of this $A$ is also a plane and not the whole 3D space
    \item The square matrix $A$ has no inverse. Its determinant is zero. It is unusual.
\end{itemize}

\bigskip

\section*{Subspaces of Vector Spaces}

In Chapters 1 to 4, the organizing ideas are \textbf{vector spaces}. The columns of $A$ are in
$m$-dimensional space $\mathbf{R}^m$. The rows are in $n$-dimensional space $\mathbf{R}^n$. But the action is in
\textbf{four subspaces} inside $\mathbf{R}^m$ and $\mathbf{R}^n$. The matrix $A$ multiplies $x_{\text{row}}$ in its \emph{row space} and $x_{\text{null}}$
in its \emph{nullspace}. The output is $Ax_{\text{row}} = b$ in its \emph{column space} because $Ax_{\text{null}} = \text{zero}$.
Then the complete solution to $Ax = b$ has a row space part and a nullspace part:

$$
\boxed{
x = x_{\text{row}} + \text{any } x_{\text{null}}
\qquad
Ax = Ax_{\text{row}} + Ax_{\text{null}} = b + 0 = b
}
$$

\bigskip

\emph{Every linear equation $Ax = b$ is solved this way: particular $x_{\text{row}}$ + any homogeneous $x_{\text{null}}$.}

\bigskip

\section*{The Plan for the Book}

That example is part of a new start. I believe it is a better start (for the reader and the
course). By working with specific matrices to introduce the algebra, the subject unfolds.
Chapter 1 develops the matrix equation $A = C$ times $R$. $C$ takes independent vectors
like $a_1$ and $a_2$ from the column space of $A$. $R$ takes independent vectors from the row
space of $A$. Those two ``vector spaces'' are at the center of linear algebra. We meet them
properly in Chapter 3. But you will know about independence from examples in Chapter 1.

\bigskip

\emph{The big step is to factor $A$ into $C$ times $R$.} Matrix multiplication is a crucial operation,
and Chapter 1 ends with four different ways to do it---seen on the back cover of the book.
This sets the path to all the great factorizations of linear algebra:

\bigskip

\begin{tabbing}
$A = LU$ \qquad\qquad \= Chapter 2 solves $n$ equations $Ax = b$ in $n$ unknowns: $A$ is square \\[6pt]
$A = CR$ \> Chapter 3 reduces to $r$ independent columns and $r$ independent rows \\[6pt]
$A = QR$ \> Chapter 4 changes the columns of $A$ into perpendicular columns of $Q$ \\[6pt]
$S = Q\Lambda Q^{\mathrm{T}}$ \> Chapter 6 has \textbf{eigenvectors} in $Q$. The eigenvalues of $S$ are in $\Lambda$ \\[6pt]
$A = U\Sigma V^{\mathrm{T}}$ \> Chapter 7 has \textbf{singular vectors} in $U$ and $V$ and \textbf{singular values} in $\Sigma$
\end{tabbing}

\bigskip

The columns of $A$ are in $m$-dimensional space $\mathbf{R}^m$, the rows are in $\mathbf{R}^n$. The $m$ by $n$ matrix
multiplies vectors $x$ in $\mathbf{R}^n$ to produce $Ax$ in $\mathbf{R}^m$. But the real action of $A$ is seen in the
four fundamental subspaces.

\bigskip

Chapter 2 only allows one solution $x_{\text{row}}$. The matrix $A$ is square and invertible.
Chapter 3 finds every solution to $Ax = b$ by adding every $x_{\text{null}}$. Chapter 4 deals with equations
that don't have a solution (because $b$ has a piece from the mysterious fourth subspace).
I hope you will like the ``big picture of linear algebra'' on page 124: \textbf{all four subspaces}.

\bigskip

Those five factorizations are a perfect way to organize and remember linear algebra.
The eigenvalues in the matrix $\Lambda$ and the singular values in $\Sigma$ come from $S$ and $A$
in a beautiful way---but not a simple way. Those numbers in $Sx = \lambda x$ and $Av = \sigma u$ see
deeply into the symmetric matrix $S$ and the $m$ by $n$ matrix $A$. Often $S$ appears in engineering
and physics. Often $A$ is a matrix of data. And data is now coming from everywhere.

\bigskip

Please don't miss Tim Baumann's page 272 on compressing photographs by the SVD.

\bigskip

Chapter 5 explains the amazing formulas produced by determinants. Amazing but
unfortunately difficult to compute! We solve equations $Ax = b$ before (not after) we
find the determinant of $A$. Those equations ask us to produce $b$ from the columns of $A$.

$$
Ax = x_1 (\text{column } 1) + x_2 (\text{column } 2) + \cdots + x_n (\text{column } n) = \textbf{ right side } b.
$$

\bigskip

In principle, determinants lead to eigenvalues (Chapter 6) and singular values (Chapter 7).
In practice, we look for other ways to find and use those important numbers. And yet
determinants tell us about geometry too---like the volume of a tilted box in $n$ dimensions.

\bigskip

A short course can go directly from dimensions in 3.5 to eigenvalues for $2 \times 2$ matrices.

\section*{Final Chapter: Learning from Data}

We added Chapter 8 on \textbf{data science}. The data often comes in a rectangular matrix $A$.
Each row measures $n$ properties of a sample. That number $n$ is large, and the number of
samples is often very large. Out of this big matrix, applied linear algebra has to find out
what is important. The goal is to produce understanding that leads to a decision.

\bigskip

In machine learning the output is a \textbf{nonlinear function of the input}. Deep learning
aims to find that function from the training data. It produces (often with giant computa-
tions) a learning function $F(x, v)$. The input vectors $v$ contain the features of each training
sample (many $v$'s from many samples). The vector $x$ contains the weights assigned to those
features. And hidden inside $F$ is a nonlinear function that mixes the inputs and the weights.
A favorite is the simple function $\text{ReLU}(y) = \text{maximum of } 0 \text{ and } y$: a \emph{ramp function}.

\bigskip

Optimizing the weights to learn from the training data $v$ is the big computation.
When that is well done, we can input new samples that the system has never seen.
The success of deep learning is that $F(x, v)$ is often close to the correct output.
The system has identified an image, or translated a sentence, or chosen a winning move.

\bigskip

This is \emph{piecewise linear algebra}, a mixture of weight matrices and ReLU. It is included
with no expectation of testing students. This is a chapter to use later, in any way you want.
You could experiment with the website \texttt{playground.tensorflow.org}.

\bigskip

Machine learning has become important and powerful---based on linear algebra and
calculus (optimizing the weights) and on statistics (controlling the mean and variance).
\emph{But it is not required or even expected to be part of the course. What I hope is that the
faster start allows you to reach eigenvalues and singular values---those are true highlights
of this subject.} Chapters 2 to 7 confirm the jump of intuition near the end of Chapter 1:

\bigskip

\begin{quote}
If all columns of $A$ lie on an $r$-dimensional plane, then all rows of $A$ also lie on a
(usually different) $r$-dimensional plane. That fact has far-reaching consequences.
\end{quote}

\bigskip

This is a textbook for a normal linear algebra class---to explain the key ideas of this
beautiful subject to \emph{everyone}. As clearly as I can. Thank you.

\bigskip
\hfill Gilbert Strang

\bigskip

After writing this introduction, I looked back at the opening example. That 3 by 3 matrix
$A$ has dependent columns (column $1$ + column $2$ = column $3$). By linear algebra,
$A$ must also have dependent rows. The numbers to show this are $-5$ and $3$:

$$
-5(\text{row } 1) + 3(\text{row } 2) = \text{row } 3 \text{ of } A
\qquad\qquad
-5(1,2,3) + 3(3,4,7) = (4,2,6).
$$

\bigskip

The number of independent rows equals the number of independent columns. \emph{Wonderful!}

\section*{Computational Linear Algebra}

We need to solve large problems quickly and accurately. Linear algebra is often the key to
computations in engineering and science and management and finance. This page aims to
provide an update on hard power and soft power---computers and math.

\bigskip

The 500 fastest computers in the world are listed on \textbf{top500.org/news}. The leaders are
mostly paid for and controlled by governments, and we can give their locations: Kobe,
Oak Ridge, Livermore, Wuxi, Guangzhou. The next group includes Italy, Switzerland,
Germany, France, Korea, Australia, Taiwan, UAE, UK, Russia, Spain, Saudi Arabia,
Finland, Norway, and Brazil. The scientific interest is in their speed and special processors.
A good source of information is \textbf{top500.org/resources/frequently-asked-questions}.

\bigskip

The benchmark tests are linear algebra problems---factoring a big matrix into $L$ times
$U$ (lower triangular times upper triangular) and then solving a system of linear equations
$Ax = b$. The actual test is in the High Performance LINPACK benchmark. Those prob-
lems are at the heart of Chapters 2 and 3, now speeded up by parallel processing.

\bigskip

The top machine achieves $415$ petaflops $= 415$ times $10^{15}$ double precision floating
point operations per second. This is with extremely careful coding for special hardware.
The important point is that ordinary computers have also seen a tremendous increase in
speed. And there are comparable savings from better algorithms.

\bigskip

\section*{Numerical Linear Algebra}

This is the subject of major research: \emph{fast algorithms for matrix computations}. The first
was thousands of years ago with ``elimination''. For that idea and any new one, part of
the test is to count the steps: in this case $n^3/3$ for $n$ linear equations with $n$ unknowns.
To compute eigenvalues and now singular values, big progress brought that also to $cn^3$.
Favorite textbooks among many good ones are

\begin{itemize}
    \item L. N. Trefethen and David Bau, \emph{Numerical Linear Algebra}, SIAM (1997)
    \item Gene Golub and Charles van Loan, \emph{Matrix Computations}, Johns Hopkins (2013).
\end{itemize}

For the mathematics of linear algebra (not focused on computation) we mention

\begin{itemize}
    \item Roger Horn and Charles Johnson, \emph{Matrix Analysis}, Cambridge (2013)
    \item R. Bhatia, \emph{Matrix Analysis}, Springer and \emph{Positive Definite Matrices}, Princeton
\end{itemize}

\bigskip

\section*{Randomized Numerical Linear Algebra}

The usual algorithms meet a barrier when the problem becomes very large. We can hardly
store and read all the data. Even matrix multiplication $AB$ becomes impossible. Eigenval-
ues hit their own barrier at matrix size $n = 10^4$ or $10^5$.

\bigskip

A solution has been found if the matrix has enough structure. Random samples
tell us about the matrix! This is a deep and beautiful subject---see a masterpiece by
P. G. Martinsson and J. A. Tropp in \emph{Acta Numerica} (2020) and arXiv: 2002.01387.
