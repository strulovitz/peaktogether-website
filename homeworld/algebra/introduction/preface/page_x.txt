\bigskip

I hope this book will be useful to you long after the linear algebra course is complete.
It is all the applications of linear algebra that make this possible. Matrices carry data,
and other matrices \emph{operate on that data}. The goal is to ``see into a matrix'' by understand-
ing its eigenvalues and eigenvectors and singular values and singular vectors. And each
application has special matrices---here are four examples:

\bigskip

\begin{tabbing}
\hspace{1cm}\textbf{Markov matrices $M$} \hspace{1.5cm} \= Each column is a set of probabilities adding to 1. \\[0.5cm]
\textbf{Incidence matrices $A$} \> Graphs and networks start with a set of nodes. The matrix \\
\> $A$ tells the \emph{connections} (edges) between those nodes. \\[0.5cm]
\textbf{Transform matrices $F$} \> The Fourier matrix uncovers the \emph{frequencies} in the data. \\[0.5cm]
\textbf{Covariance matrices $C$} \> The \textbf{variance} is key information about a random variable. \\
\> \emph{The covariance explains dependence between variables.}
\end{tabbing}

\bigskip

We included those applications and more in this Sixth Edition. For the crucial computation
of matrix weights in deep learning, Chapter 9 presents the ideas of \textbf{optimization}. This is
where linear algebra meets calculus: \textbf{derivative = zero} becomes a matrix equation at the
minimum point because $F(x)$ has many variables.

Here is a small touch of linear algebra---three questions before this course gets serious:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Suppose you draw three straight line segments of lengths $r$ and $s$ and $t$ on this page.
    What are the conditions on those three lengths to allow you to make the segments
    into a triangle? In this question you can choose the directions of the three lines.

    \item Now suppose the directions of three straight lines $u, v, w$ are fixed and different.
    But you could stretch those lines to $au, bv, cw$ with any numbers $a, b, c$.
    Can you always make a closed triangle out of the three vectors $au, bv, cw$?

    \item Linear algebra doesn't stay in a plane! Suppose you have \textbf{four lines} $u, v, w, z$
    in different directions in 3-dimensional space. Can you always choose the numbers
    $a, b, c, d$ (zeros not allowed) so that $au + bv + cw + dz = 0$?
\end{enumerate}
