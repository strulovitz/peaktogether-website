\bigskip

\begin{center}
{\Large \textbf{Five Factorizations of a Matrix}}
\end{center}

\bigskip

Here are the organizing principles of linear algebra. When our matrix has a special property,
these factorizations will show it. Chapter after chapter, they express the key idea in a direct
and useful way.

\bigskip

The usefulness increases as you go down the list. \textbf{Orthogonal matrices} are the win-
ners in the end, because their columns are perpendicular unit vectors. That is perfection.

$$
\text{2 by 2 Orthogonal Matrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{bmatrix} = \text{Rotation by Angle } \theta
$$

\bigskip

\textbf{Here are the five factorizations from Chapters 1, 2, 4, 6, 7:}

\bigskip

\begin{tabbing}
\hspace{0.3cm}\textbf{1} \hspace{1cm} \= $A = CR$ \hspace{1cm} \= $=$ \ $R$ combines independent columns in $C$ to give all columns of $A$ \\[0.4cm]
\textbf{2} \> $A = LU$ \> $=$ \ Lower triangular $L$ times Upper triangular $U$ \\[0.4cm]
\textbf{4} \> $A = QR$ \> $=$ \ Orthogonal matrix $Q$ times Upper triangular $R$ \\[0.4cm]
\textbf{6} \> $S = Q\Lambda Q^{\mathrm{T}}$ \> $=$ \ (Orthogonal $Q$) (Eigenvalues in $\Lambda$) (Orthogonal $Q^{\mathrm{T}}$) \\[0.4cm]
\textbf{7} \> $A = U\Sigma V^{\mathrm{T}}$ \> $=$ \ (Orthogonal $U$) (Singular values in $\Sigma$) (Orthogonal $V^{\mathrm{T}}$) \\
\end{tabbing}

\bigskip

May I call your attention to the last one? It is the \textbf{Singular Value Decomposition (SVD)}.
It applies to every matrix $A$. Those factors $U$ and $V$ have perpendicular columns---all of
length one. Multiplying any vector by $U$ or $V$ leaves a vector of the same length---so
computations don't blow up or down. And $\Sigma$ is a positive diagonal matrix of ``singular
values''. If you learn about eigenvalues and eigenvectors in Chapter 6, \emph{please} continue
a few pages to singular values in Section 7.1.

\bigskip

\begin{center}
{\Large \textbf{Deep Learning}}
\end{center}

\bigskip

For a true picture of linear algebra, applications have to be included. Completeness is
totally impossible. At this moment, the dominating direction of applied mathematics
has one special requirement: \emph{It cannot be entirely linear!}

\bigskip

One name for that direction is ``deep learning''. It is an extremely successful approach to
a fundamental scientific problem: \textbf{Learning from data}. In many cases the data comes in a
matrix. Our goal is to look inside the matrix for the connections between variables. Instead
of solving matrix equations or differential equations that express known input-output rules,
\emph{we have to find those rules}. The success of deep learning is to build a function $F(x, v)$
with inputs $x$ and $v$ of two kinds:

\bigskip

\begin{quote}
The vectors $v$ describes the features of the training data.

\medskip
The matrices $x$ assign weights to those features.

\medskip
The function $F(x, v)$ is close to the correct output for that training data $v$.

\medskip
\textbf{When $v$ changes to unseen test data, $F(x, v)$ stays close to correct.}
\end{quote}

\bigskip

This success comes partly from the form of the learning function $F$, which allows it to
include vast amounts of data. In the end, a linear function $F$ would be totally inadequate.
The favorite choice for $F$ is \textbf{piecewise linear}. This combines simplicity with generality.
