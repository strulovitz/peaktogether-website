\noindent \textbf{viii} \hfill \textit{Introduction to Linear Algebra}

\bigskip

\begin{center}
{\Large \textbf{The Four Fundamental Subspaces}}
\end{center}

\bigskip

You have just seen how the course begins---with the columns of a matrix $A$. There were
two key steps. One step was to take all combinations $ca_1 + da_2 + ea_3 + fa_4$ of the
columns. This led to the \textbf{column space of $A$}. The other step was to \textbf{factor the matrix into
$C$ times $R$}. That matrix $C$ holds a full set of \textbf{independent columns}.

\bigskip

I fully recognize that this is only the Preface to the book. You have had zero practice
with the column space of a matrix (and even less practice with $C$ and $R$). But the good
thing is: Those are the right directions to start. Eventually, \emph{every matrix will lead to four
fundamental spaces}. Together with the column space of $A$ comes the \textbf{row space---all
combinations of the rows}. When we take all combinations of the $n$ columns and all
combinations of the $m$ rows---those combinations fill up ``spaces'' of vectors.

\bigskip

The other two subspaces complete the picture. Suppose the row space is a plane in
three dimensions. Then there is one special direction in the 3D picture---\textbf{that direction is
perpendicular to the row space}. That perpendicular line is the \textbf{nullspace} of the matrix.
We will see that the vectors in the nullspace (perpendicular to all the rows) solve $Ax = 0$:
the most basic of linear equations.

\bigskip

And if vectors perpendicular to all the rows are important, so are the vectors
perpendicular to all the columns. Here is the picture of the \textbf{Four Fundamental Subspaces}.

\noindent \textbf{The Four Fundamental Subspaces: An $m$ by $n$ matrix with $r$ independent columns.}

\bigskip

This picture of four subspaces comes in Chapter 3. The idea of perpendicular spaces is
developed in Chapter 4. And special ``basis vectors'' for all four subspaces are discovered
in Chapter 7. That step is the final piece in the \textbf{Fundamental Theorem of Linear Algebra}.
The theorem includes an amazing fact about any matrix, square or rectangular:

\begin{center}
\textbf{The number of independent columns equals the number of independent rows.}
\end{center}
