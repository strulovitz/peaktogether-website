Path 7: Composite Models (Technicolor and Preons)

Make known particles less fundamental — explained by deeper constituents or strong dynamics.

Idea

Instead of adding higher symmetries or dimensions, make the known particles less fundamental. In technicolor models, the Higgs boson’s role is played by bound states of new strong interactions (analogous to how pions arise from QCD), so the electroweak force would unify with a new strong force at some scale. In preon models, quarks and leptons themselves are bound states of more basic entities (preons), potentially held together by a new force. The hope is that a deeper layer of constituents and forces could explain the patterns in the Standard Model and even reduce the number of fundamental forces.

Rationale

The Standard Model’s pattern of three families of quarks and leptons and a plethora of parameters has long suggested a substructure – similar to how the periodic table hinted at atoms, and protons/neutrons suggested quarks. Preon theories (1970s–80s) attempted to unify quarks and leptons by positing a few fundamental preons that bind in different combinations to give all fermions. If successful, this would unify matter content and potentially unify interactions if, for example, the binding force for preons also somehow related to gauge forces. Technicolor, introduced in the late 1970s, tried to solve the gauge hierarchy problem by replacing the Higgs mechanism with a new QCD-like force that “unifies” with the electroweak sector – the strongly bound techni-particles break electroweak symmetry without a fundamental scalar. These approaches are quite distinct, but both remove the fundamental status of some pieces of the Standard Model in favor of a deeper theory, thereby addressing some “unexplained coincidences” (like weak mixing angles, mass hierarchies) via dynamics.

Prerequisite Themes

Strong interaction theory and confinement (QCD as an analog for technicolor); Spontaneous chiral symmetry breaking (pions as Nambu–Goldstone bosons, analogous to Higgs as a pseudo-Goldstone in technicolor); Preon model basics (hypothetical subcomponents of quarks/leptons, binding rules); Experimental constraints on substructure (high-energy scattering limits on compositeness).

Dependencies

Composite approaches are somewhat alternative to high-energy unification approaches – for instance, technicolor eschews a fundamental Higgs and thus doesn’t require supersymmetry to stabilize its mass. Preon models could be pursued alongside a GUT (some early GUT proposals considered whether families could be composite) but generally these paths run against the idea of a single high-energy simple group. They also don’t inherently include gravity. They might interact with Path 6 if one imagines string theory might produce composite effective particles.

Signs of Progress

Evidence of quark or lepton substructure would be game-changing. This could appear as deviations in high-energy scattering: if quarks/leptons are composite, at sufficiently high momentum transfer their form factors would show it (much like Rutherford scattering revealed nuclei). Experiments at the LHC and lepton colliders have tested this up to multi-TeV scales and so far seen no hint of finite size or excited states, pushing any substructure scale above ~10 TeV. Discovery of a “excited lepton” or “excited quark” (a heavier version of a lepton/quark that is presumably an excited bound state of preons) would confirm preons. For technicolor, signs would include the observation of the new resonances (techni-rho mesons, etc.) or deviations in the Higgs properties (since the Higgs in SM would be composite or replaced). The LHC’s discovery of a light, Standard-Model-like Higgs and no other resonances has mostly ruled out simplest technicolor models, which tended to predict a heavier composite Higgs and additional particles. If future colliders find that the Higgs has substructure or detect a new strong interaction in electroweak processes, it would resurrect this path.

Base Camp 7.1: Strong Interaction Analogies

Scope: Review how the known strong force (QCD) causes composites (hadrons) and what lessons it provides for new strong forces.

Stepping-stones: (a) QCD confinement: quarks are bound by SU(3) color, no free quarks observed; at low energies the coupling grows and quarks form hadrons. (b) Chiral symmetry breaking: QCD has approximate $SU(2)_L \times SU(2)_R$ chiral symmetry which is spontaneously broken to $SU(2)_V$, resulting in pions as Goldstone bosons (and they’re pseudo-Goldstone due to small quark masses). The nucleon mass arises not from current quark mass but from QCD dynamics (~GeV scale). This analogy is important for technicolor (where W and Z get mass like nucleons do). (c) Scaling/Running: QCD has asymptotic freedom (coupling weak at high energies, strong at low). The concept of a $\Lambda$ scale (e.g. $\Lambda_{\text{QCD}}\sim 200$ MeV) where confinement sets in. A new strong force would have its own $\Lambda_{\text{TC}}$ or scale. (d) Hadron spectrum: Bound states (mesons, baryons) spectrum in QCD – often the lightest is a pseudoscalar (pion), then vector (rho). In technicolor, the analogs would be technipions (some of which might be “eaten” by W/Z) and techni-rho resonances.

Resources:

  • David Gross – “The Discovery of Asymptotic Freedom and the Emergence of QCD,” Proc. Natl. Acad. Sci. USA 102 (2005) 9099. Why: A reflective piece by Gross explaining asymptotic freedom and how QCD became established. It’s useful to read to understand the significance of strong coupling behavior and how analogies might carry to new forces. Gross explains the running of the coupling elegantly, which is central to technicolor ideas.
  • Anthony Hey & Patrick Walters – The New Quantum Universe (Cambridge Univ. Press, 2003), Chapters on quark confinement. Why: This is a semi-popular book that explains quark confinement, chiral symmetry, etc., in accessible language with some math. It sets up the stage for someone new to these concepts to appreciate what technicolor tries to leverage (i.e., the natural mass scale generation in QCD-like theories).
  • Frank Wilczek – “Scaling Mount Planck II: Composite Weak Bosons,” Physics Today 42(11) (1989), p. 13. Why: A short thought piece by Wilczek discussing the possibility that W and Z bosons are composite, drawing analogies to rho mesons in QCD. It’s quite lucid and gives insight into why people thought technicolor could be viable. This bridges nicely from known strong force to speculative new strong dynamics.

Base Camp 7.2: Technicolor Theory

Scope: Learn the basics of technicolor: what is the new gauge group, what are techni-fermions, and how do they break electroweak symmetry.

Stepping-stones: (a) Minimal technicolor: assume a new $SU(N)_{\text{TC}}$ gauge interaction with some doublet of techni-fermions (analog of quark doublet) such that $\langle \bar{T}T \rangle \neq 0$ condensate forms at scale $\Lambda_{\text{TC}} \sim$ a few hundred GeV, which spontaneously breaks $SU(2)_L \times U(1)_Y$ to $U(1)_{\text{EM}}$ (just like $\bar{q}q$ condensate breaks chiral symmetry). The techni-pions are “eaten” to become the longitudinal $W^\pm$ and $Z^0$. (b) Scale and Fermi constant: relate the condensate scale to $v = 246$ GeV: e.g. $F_{\pi_{\text{TC}}} \approx 246$ GeV (the technipion decay constant must equal the electroweak scale). (c) Technirho and other resonances: expected bound states like technirho, techniomega, etc., potentially around a few TeV, which could be produced at colliders as new resonances coupling to electroweak currents (since they mix with W/Z). (d) Extended technicolor (ETC): minimal technicolor only gives masses to W/Z. Quark and lepton masses require interactions connecting SM fermions to techni-fermions (ETC gauge bosons at some high scale $\Lambda_{\text{ETC}}$ that give four-fermion operators $\bar{f}f\bar{T}T$ leading to $m_f \sim \frac{\langle \bar{T}T \rangle}{\Lambda_{\text{ETC}}^2}$). Understand this mechanism and the problem: generating heavy top mass would require relatively low $\Lambda_{\text{ETC}}$ which tends to induce flavor-changing neutral currents (FCNCs) unacceptably. (e) Walking technicolor: mention that if the coupling runs slowly (“walks”), condensates can be enhanced and FCNC suppressed potentially – this was a proposed solution to the flavor problem.

Resources:

  • Estia Eichten & Kenneth Lane – “Dynamical Breaking of Weak Interaction Symmetries,” Phys. Lett. B 90 (1980), p. 125. Why: A foundational paper setting out the technicolor idea clearly. It’s a short letter but describes how a technifermion condensate breaks electroweak symmetry and gives an overview of extended technicolor for masses. Seeing the original reasoning by Eichten & Lane (who were pioneers) is valuable.
  • Kenneth Lane – “Technicolor 2000,” (arXiv:hep-ph/0007304). Why: A comprehensive but pedagogical review by one of technicolor’s main proponents, written around 2000. It covers basics of technicolor, extended technicolor, walking technicolor, and experimental constraints up to that time. It’s conversational in style and addresses the successes and issues frankly. It’s excellent for a learner to understand both how technicolor works and why it’s hard to make it realistic.
  • S. Weinberg – “Implications of Dynamical Symmetry Breaking,” Phys. Rev. D 13 (1976), p. 974. Why: One of the earliest papers suggesting what became technicolor (Weinberg and also Susskind independently in ’79 proposed such ideas). Weinberg’s perspective is always enlightening. This paper is a bit formal, but the introduction gives the concept: gauge symmetries broken by new strong dynamics. It’s historically interesting and shows how the concept was born as an alternative to elementary Higgs.

Base Camp 7.3: Preon Models and Quark-Lepton Compositeness

Scope: Investigate models where quarks and leptons are made of smaller entities, and how they try to account for the spectrum of Standard Model.

Stepping-stones: (a) Preon basics: assume a few fundamental constituents (“preons”) and binding by a new gauge force (“metacolor”). For example, the Pati–Salam preon model had 2 preons making up all types of quarks/leptons in different combinations. (b) Binding mechanism: what force could bind preons? It might need to be very strong at an extremely high scale to confine preons into particles of masses ~GeV or heavier. (c) Spectrum issues: combining preons to get spin-1/2 quarks and leptons usually also yields unwanted composite states (like a whole slew of excited states or even composites with wrong quantum numbers). Models had to fine-tune to avoid a super-rich spectrum which we don’t see. (d) Level of composite-ness: if quark is composite of preons, there might be excited quark states with mass scale related to binding energy; what scale can that be given experiments (currently a few TeV at least). (e) Preons & GUT vs. preons vs. technicolor: realize differences – preons unify matter, whereas GUT unified interactions; some preon models came with their own gauge unification (like Harari–Shupe model with a flavor-unified preon). Also, how preons fell out of favor: the more we probed, the more quarks looked point-like up to >10^-19 m.

Resources:

  • Particle Data Group (PDG) Review – “Hypothetical Particles: Preons” (find in PDG around mid-1980s editions). Why: The older PDG editions often had short review sections on speculative topics like preons. These were written to summarize the state of such models and experimental limits. If accessible, a PDG blurb would concisely list preon model classes and limits on compositeness scale from data (e.g. e^- scattering).
  • Haim Harari – “A Schematic Model of Quarks and Leptons,” Phys. Lett. B 86 (1979), p. 83. Why: A classic preon model by Harari (and independently Shupe) that proposed each generation’s fermions could be formed by two preons in different combinations. It’s a short paper – reading it lets you see the simplicity aimed for and think why such a neat pattern didn’t materialize (or what assumptions were needed).
  • J. L. Rosner – “Status of Compositeness (preons),” Comments Nucl. Part. Phys. 15 (1986) 195. Why: A review article by Rosner, after a decade of preon model building, discussing which features work and which don’t, and summarizing experimental bounds circa mid-80s. It’s valuable for its critical analysis. Although old, many arguments remain: e.g. the challenge of explaining three generations and their mass splitting, and the notorious problem that composite models tended to either violate spin-statistics or needed forces that were hard to reconcile with known physics.

Base Camp 7.4: Experimental Limits on Compositeness

Scope: Summarize how experiments have searched for signs that quarks or leptons have size or substructure, and for technicolor resonances, etc.

Stepping-stones: (a) Deep inelastic scattering data: from HERA (ep collider) and earlier (SLAC in the 60s) which established quarks as point-like down to $10^{-19}$ m. Understand how the structure functions match point particles and what deviation a finite size would cause (like a form factor fall-off). (b) Contact interactions: in absence of explicit resonances, compositeness can cause four-fermion contact terms (like $(\bar{q}\gamma^\mu q)(\bar{\ell}\gamma_\mu \ell)$) when preons exchanged – parameterize by a compositeness scale $\Lambda$. LEP and Tevatron/LHC have set limits on these (often $\Lambda \gt$ tens of TeV). (c) Excited fermion searches: e.g. an excited electron $e^*$ decaying to $e+\gamma$ would give a peak – experiments have looked, nothing seen up to certain masses. Summarize typical mass limits (~TeV range). (d) Technicolor resonances: mention LEP’s precise measurements of W and Z properties severely constrained technicolor (technirho mixing with W would shift parameters), and direct LHC searches for technirho -> W+Z or techipions etc. All have yielded no discovery, pushing technirho mass if exists beyond ~2-3 TeV. (e) Current status: basically, there’s no evidence of compositeness or new strong dynamics up to multi-TeV, which either means these models have to be deeply hidden (like very high scale or very subtle), or nature chose a fundamental Higgs after all.

Resources:

  • PDG Review – “Searches for Quark and Lepton Compositeness,” in PDG 2022 (or recent). Why: PDG regularly includes a section summarizing limits on compositeness scale from various processes. It provides tables of limits for different channels (eeqq, etc.) and is a concise authoritative summary.
  • ATLAS Collaboration – “Search for high-mass dilepton resonances and contact interactions in pp collisions at √s = 13 TeV with the ATLAS detector,” Phys. Lett. B 796 (2019), p. 68. Why: This paper sets limits on contact interactions (compositeness) as well as dilepton resonances. It can serve as an example of how LHC data translates to a $\Lambda$ compositeness limit (often on order 20-30 TeV). Reading the introduction and results, one can extract current bounds.
  • CDF Collaboration – “Search for Technicolor Particles in Exclusive Multilepton Events,” Phys. Rev. Lett. 64 (1990), p. 176. Why: A result from early Tevatron searching for technicolor (specifically technirho decays). It’s historical, showing how experiments looked for multi-lepton signals that technicolor models predicted (through technipion decays). Including it to illustrate that technicolor was actively searched for decades, and one can mention that progressively higher energy experiments (D0, ATLAS/CMS) continued and pushed limits further out.
