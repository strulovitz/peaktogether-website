Subject: Story: I protested against AI for ten years. I can't write a line of code. Then AI built my life's work — a free "Disneyland of mathematics" (playable today, open-source)

Dear [JOURNALIST NAME],

I'll give you the whole story in one paragraph, and then I'll unpack it. For a decade I was an anti-AI activist — I wrote a protest book, and recorded parody protest songs on my electric guitar, warning that this technology could be the end of us. I also cannot program at all: I can't write "Hello World," and I have ADD. And yet over the past month, I directed AI to build something I believe has never existed before: a free, open-source "Disneyland of mathematics" — a virtual theme park where loving remakes of the classic '90s games Descent, Quake, Loom, and Homeworld secretly teach real university mathematics, two players on one screen, no signup, no ads, nothing for sale, ever. It began as an anniversary gift for my girlfriend. Every sentence in this paragraph is true, every one of them is verifiable in one click, and I am a nobody from Israel with no PR agency — I'm sending this letter to many newsrooms at once, and whoever publishes first is simply first.

Let me show you what a visitor actually sees. In one game, two players fly a spaceship through robots-infested corridors where the locked doors are the actual steps of a 300-year-old mathematical proof — and the weapons are named Euler, Newton, and Riemann: to open a door, you fire the mathematician whose idea it was. In another, Newton's Principia — the book that invented physics — has been rebuilt as a Quake dungeon, room by room, from the original 1687 figures; you "read" the walls by shooting them, and they bloom from black-and-white into color. In a third, you don't see the mathematics at all: you hear it — a landscape is played by a 13-instrument orchestra, and couples find a mountain pass together by ear, because a peak sounds like one bright chord and a saddle sounds eerie and stretched. In a fourth, a fleet of spaceships IS a mathematical matrix, and commanding the fleet is — without the player quite noticing — a university linear algebra course. Each game page has a one-minute gameplay video that autoplays the moment you open it; you can judge everything I just said in about five minutes, at peaktogether.me. But the site itself is only half the story. The other half is that, by every normal rule, the person who made it had no right to be able to.

I am not being modest: I genuinely cannot code. Not a line. What I did was direct — I described, argued, tested, rejected, and demanded, and AI wrote every line of code and every line of mathematics: the 3D engines, the custom shaders, the real-time orchestra synthesized from 89 Philharmonia samples, the faithful historical proofs. Right now, your industry is having a loud argument about whether "vibe-coding" — non-programmers building software by directing AI — is a revolution or a toy. I am a data point in that argument that anyone can download: a single person with no training, no funding, no team, and a diagnosed attention disorder, holding what used to require a studio. Every commit is public on GitHub; the receipts are total. And the reason I started building it is the part of the story my own mother wouldn't believe.

It started with conversations, not code. My girlfriend and I would talk — really talk — about things like Ramanujan's scandalous equation (the one that says 1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + … somehow equals −1/12), or about the physicist David Bohm, whose life Oppenheimer helped ruin. She isn't a mathematician; math frightens her, like most normal people. But those conversations were so much fun that I had an idea: what if there were a romantic activity for couples built out of the most beautiful ideas humans have ever had? Then, for our first anniversary, I made her a surprise: I vibe-coded a little 3D world where she could fly — she's a gamer, so she got a real flight joystick — over the places of her dreams, the Eiffel Tower, a Japanese shrine, while a tiny plane skywrote love messages behind the monuments. She loved it. I took the joystick code from her gift and connected it to the mathematics, and the theme park was born from there — one game at a time. She stays out of this story by her own choice — she is a very private person, and I ask that she remain so — but she is the reason every game on that site is built for two players on one screen, sharing one keyboard, forced to talk to each other. The site's whole philosophy is hers, even the honesty: when she told me the early version was confusing, I rebuilt everything games-first. She loves me, not the games — and that sentence, I think, explains the project better than any press release could. Which brings me to the twist I promised: why a man like me spent ten years fighting the very technology that made all this possible.

From 2014 — before it was fashionable — I warned publicly that self-improving machines could end humanity. I wrote a book about it (Anti AI Rebellion, free on the Internet Archive), full of protest art: parody songs, musicals, screenplays, all against self-replicating technology, performed on my own guitar and still sitting on my YouTube channel. I have not changed my mind about the danger. Both things are simply true at once: I believe this technology may be the most dangerous thing we have ever built, and it is the only thing that ever gave someone like me — no degree, no team, no ability to code — the power to build something beautiful for the person I love. I don't resolve that contradiction. I live inside it. If 2026 has a defining feeling, I think that's it.

Why might your readers care? Because the site is free and stays free — open-source, no accounts, no ads, a genuine gift to the commons in an internet that has almost forgotten what that looks like. Because it's built two-players-on-one-couch on purpose, against the loneliness of screens. Because underneath the arcade there's a bigger architecture — every famous unsolved problem in mathematics is a "mountain" on the site, with the games as climbing routes, and the same AI-directing recipe I used is published openly so that anyone — a teacher, a student, a boyfriend planning a birthday gift — can build the next exhibit without knowing how to code. And because the story of how it was made is, I'd argue, a preview of how a lot of things are about to be made.

Everything checks out in minutes: the site is live at peaktogether.me (a one-minute silent gameplay video autoplays on every game page), the full source code and commit history are at https://github.com/strulovitz/peaktogether-website/(opens in new tab), the protest book is at https://archive.org/details/anti-ai-rebellion-protest-against-artificial-intelligence(opens in new tab), and the protest songs are at https://www.youtube.com/@StrulovitzNir/playlists(opens in new tab). I am available immediately for interviews — on camera, gladly — in English, from Israel, any time zone. Photos of me available on request; my partner remains anonymous.

Thank you for reading this far. I promised you at the top that every sentence was true. Now it's one click to check.
