Subject: Story: The hardest unsolved problems in science — from the Riemann Hypothesis to curing cancer — rebuilt as a free theme park anyone can climb

Dear journalist,

I'll give you the whole story in one paragraph, and then I'll unpack it. There is now a virtual theme park built around thirteen of the hardest unsolved problems on Earth — the Riemann Hypothesis, Navier–Stokes, turbulence, quantum gravity, room-temperature superconductors, artificial photosynthesis, the origin of life, aging, a cure for cancer — where each problem is a "mountain," each mountain has fully-researched base camps a lay person can actually climb, and the front gate is an arcade of loving remakes of '90s classics like Descent, Quake, Loom, and Homeworld, playable by two people on one screen. It is completely free, open-source, no signup, no ads, nothing for sale, ever. It was built in one month by one person — me — and I cannot program at all: I can't write "Hello World," I have ADD, and every year of high school they wanted to throw me out. I directed AI to build all of it, and it began as an anniversary gift for my girlfriend. Stranger still: before building it, I spent a decade as an anti-AI activist. Every sentence in this paragraph is true, every one is verifiable in one click, and I am a nobody from Israel with no PR agency — I'm sending this letter to many newsrooms at once, and whoever publishes first is simply first.

Start with the mountains, because that's where the new idea lives. Each of the thirteen problems — spanning mathematics, physics, chemistry, biology, and medicine — has a "sherpa": a legendary scientist who lit the path. Riemann guides his Hypothesis; Olga Ladyzhenskaya guides Navier–Stokes; Judah Folkman guides the cancer mountain; Frances Arnold guides alternative biochemistries. And each mountain has base camps: layered explanations built by running AI deep-research on the best advanced textbooks in each field, then simplifying downward, layer by layer, until a curious lay person can start from the bottom and genuinely climb — I'd argue the lowest layers are friendlier than Wikipedia itself, because they were engineered to be climbed, not merely to be correct. Everyone knows "vibe-coding" — non-programmers building software by directing AI. What I believe has never existed before is what this park attempts: call it vibe-science — the public, who don't know the equations, brought close enough to the hardest problems on the planet to genuinely engage with them. Not because a gamer will prove the Riemann Hypothesis on a Tuesday — but because of the oldest law of open source: with enough eyeballs, all bugs are shallow. Nobody knows where the next strange, decisive angle comes from; the park's bet is that widening the base of a mountain is how, eventually, somebody reaches the top. But nobody climbs a mountain they're afraid to approach — which is why the front gate of this park is not a lecture. It's an arcade.

In one game, two players fly a spaceship through the tunnels of an asteroid mine where the locked doors are the actual steps of Euler's solution to the Basel problem — why 1/1² + 1/2² + 1/3² + … equals exactly π²/6, the first base camp on the road to the Riemann Hypothesis — and the weapons are named Euler, Newton, and Riemann: to open a door, you fire the mathematician whose idea it was. In another, Newton's Principia has been rebuilt as a Quake dungeon from the original 1687 figures; you "read" the walls by shooting them, and the geometry of the greatest physics book ever written blooms from black-and-white into color. In a third, you hear multivariable calculus: a landscape performed live by a 13-instrument orchestra, where a peak sounds like one bright chord and a saddle point sounds eerie and stretched. In a fourth, a fleet of spaceships IS a matrix, and commanding it is a linear algebra course in disguise. The current games are mathematics — but the arcade's published roadmap already maps '90s classics to the other sciences: a Blockout of rotating molecules and proteins, a gravity-free pool game that becomes thermodynamics, a Dune-style strategy game where your units build and trade molecules. Each game page at peaktogether.me has a one-minute gameplay video that autoplays the moment you open it, so you can judge everything I just said in about five minutes. Now — why would anyone build such a thing? Because of two conversations.

My girlfriend and I would talk — really talk — about things like Ramanujan's scandalous equation (the one that says 1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + … somehow equals −1/12), or about David Bohm, whose pilot-wave interpretation of quantum mechanics went unheard for decades and whose life Oppenheimer helped ruin. She isn't a scientist; math frightens her, like most normal people. But those conversations were so much fun that I had an idea: what if there were a romantic activity for couples built out of the most beautiful ideas humans ever had? For our first anniversary, I made her a surprise: a little 3D world where she could fly — she's a gamer, so she got a real flight joystick — over the places of her dreams, the Eiffel Tower, a Japanese shrine, while a tiny plane skywrote love messages behind the monuments. She loved it. I took the joystick code from her gift and connected it to the mathematics, and the park grew from there. She stays out of this story by her own choice — she is a very private person, and I ask that she remain so — but she is the reason every game is built for two players on one couch, talking to each other. She loves me, not the games — and that sentence, I think, explains the project better than any press release could. But here is the part that, by every normal rule, should have been impossible.

I genuinely cannot code. Not a line. What I did was direct — I described, argued, tested, rejected, and demanded, through hundreds of rounds — and AI wrote every line of code and every line of mathematics: the 3D engines, the custom shaders, the real-time orchestra synthesized from 89 real Philharmonia samples, the historically faithful proofs, the base camps distilled from graduate textbooks. The full source and commit history are public on GitHub, so the receipts are total. Think about what that means for one moment: the kid they wanted to expel every single year can now build a climbing route up Navier–Stokes. That door is now open for everyone — every teacher, every student, every curious couple. Which brings me to the twist I promised at the top: why the man holding that door open spent ten years fighting the very technology that opened it.

From 2014 — before it was fashionable — I warned publicly that self-improving machines could end humanity. I wrote a book about it (Anti AI Rebellion, free on the Internet Archive), full of protest art: parody songs, musicals, screenplays, all against self-replicating technology, performed on my own guitar and still sitting on my YouTube channel. I have not changed my mind about the danger. Both things are simply true at once: I believe this technology may be the most dangerous thing we have ever built, and it is the only thing that ever gave someone like me the power to build something beautiful — for the person I love, and for the mountains humanity still hasn't climbed. I don't resolve that contradiction. I live inside it. If 2026 has a defining feeling, I think that's it.
