Subject: Story: The most wholesome strange thing on the internet — a free "Disneyland of mathematics" a non-coder built with AI as a gift for his girlfriend

Dear journalist,

I'll give you the whole story in one paragraph, and then I'll unpack it. There is a website where couples play '90s-style arcade games on date night — and accidentally learn real university mathematics. It's a virtual theme park: loving remakes of Descent, Quake, Loom, and Homeworld, two players on one screen, sharing one keyboard. It is completely free, open-source, no signup, no ads, nothing for sale, ever — a genuine gift to the commons, in an internet that has almost forgotten what that looks like. It was built in one month by one person — me — and here is where it gets strange: I cannot program at all. I can't write "Hello World," and I have ADD. I directed AI to write every line, as a gift for my girlfriend. And before that, I spent a decade as an anti-AI activist — protest book, protest songs, the works. Every sentence in this paragraph is true, every one is verifiable in one click, and I am a nobody from Israel with no PR agency — I'm sending this letter to many newsrooms at once, and whoever publishes first is simply first.

Let me walk you through the park, because it doesn't sound real until you see it. In one game, two players fly a spaceship through the tunnels of an asteroid mine where the locked doors are the actual steps of a 300-year-old mathematical proof — and the weapons are named Euler, Newton, and Riemann: to open a door, you fire the mathematician whose idea it was. In another, Newton's Principia — the book that invented physics — has been rebuilt as a Quake dungeon, room by room, from the original 1687 figures; you "read" the walls by shooting them, and they bloom from black-and-white into color. In a third, you don't see the mathematics at all: you hear it — a landscape is performed live by a 13-instrument orchestra, and couples find a mountain pass together by ear, because a peak sounds like one bright chord and a saddle sounds eerie and stretched. In a fourth, a fleet of spaceships IS a mathematical matrix, and commanding the fleet is — without the player quite noticing — a university linear algebra course. Each game page at peaktogether.me has a one-minute gameplay video that autoplays the moment you open it, so you can judge everything I just said in about five minutes. And the reason all of it exists is not a business plan. It's a girl.

It started with conversations, not code. My girlfriend and I would talk — really talk — about things like Ramanujan's scandalous equation (the one that says 1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + … somehow equals −1/12), or about the physicist David Bohm, whose life Oppenheimer helped ruin. She isn't a mathematician; math frightens her, like most normal people. But those conversations were so much fun that I had an idea: what if there were a romantic activity for couples built out of the most beautiful ideas humans ever had? Then, for our first anniversary, I made her a surprise: I vibe-coded a little 3D world where she could fly — she's a gamer, so she got a real flight joystick — over the places of her dreams, the Eiffel Tower, a Japanese shrine, while a tiny plane skywrote love messages behind the monuments. She loved it. I took the joystick code from her gift and connected it to the mathematics, and the theme park grew from there — one game at a time. She stays out of this story by her own choice — she is a very private person, and I ask that she remain so — but she is the reason every game is built for two players on one couch, against the loneliness of screens. She loves me, not the games — and that sentence, I think, explains the project better than any press release could. But there's a part of this story I haven't explained yet: how a man who can't write a single line of code built four 3D game engines.

I genuinely cannot code. Not a line. What I did was direct — I described, argued, tested, rejected, and demanded, through hundreds of rounds — and AI wrote every line of code and every line of mathematics: the 3D engines, the custom shaders, the real-time orchestra synthesized from 89 real Philharmonia samples, the historically faithful proofs. Right now the tech world is arguing loudly about whether "vibe-coding" — non-programmers building software by directing AI — is a revolution or a toy. I am a data point in that argument that anyone can download; the full source and commit history are public on GitHub, so the receipts are total. Which brings me to the twist I promised at the top: why a man like me spent ten years fighting the very technology that made all this possible.

From 2014 — before it was fashionable — I warned publicly that self-improving machines could end humanity. I wrote a book about it (Anti AI Rebellion, free on the Internet Archive), full of protest art: parody songs, musicals, screenplays, all against self-replicating technology, performed on my own guitar and still sitting on my YouTube channel. I have not changed my mind about the danger. Both things are simply true at once: I believe this technology may be the most dangerous thing we have ever built, and it is the only thing that ever gave someone like me the power to build something beautiful for the person I love. I don't resolve that contradiction. I live inside it. If 2026 has a defining feeling, I think that's it.

One last thing, because I suspect it's the part your readers will feel most: this site is built like the old internet. Made by one person, for love; given away whole; no growth hacks, no dark patterns, no account walls; designed for two people on one couch instead of two billion in separate feeds. And it's built to grow the way Wikipedia grew: the AI-directing recipe I used is published openly on the site, so anyone — a teacher, a student, a boyfriend planning a birthday gift — can build the next exhibit without knowing how to code. Underneath the arcade, every famous unsolved problem in mathematics is a "mountain" in the park, waiting for its climbing route. It's a theme park with room for everyone to build a ride.
