Dear journalist,

Every sentence in this paragraph is true, every one is verifiable in one click, and I am a nobody from Israel with no PR agency — I'm sending this letter to many newsrooms at once, and whoever publishes first is simply first.

Let me show you what one month of directing AI actually produced. In one game, two players fly a spaceship through the tunnels of an asteroid mine where the locked doors are the actual steps of a 300-year-old mathematical proof — and the weapons are named Euler, Newton, and Riemann: to open a door, you fire the mathematician whose idea it was. In another, Newton's Principia — the book that invented physics — has been rebuilt as a Quake dungeon, room by room, from the original 1687 figures; you "read" the walls by shooting them, and they bloom from black-and-white into color. In a third, you don't see the mathematics at all: you hear it — a landscape is performed live by a 13-instrument orchestra synthesized from 89 real Philharmonia samples, and couples find a mountain pass together by ear, because a peak sounds like one bright chord and a saddle sounds eerie and stretched. In a fourth, a fleet of spaceships IS a mathematical matrix, and commanding the fleet is — without the player quite noticing — a university linear algebra course. Custom 3D engines, custom shaders, real-time audio synthesis, historically faithful mathematics: a stack that used to require a studio. Each game page at peaktogether.me has a one-minute gameplay video that autoplays the moment you open it, so you can judge everything I just said in about five minutes. Which brings us to the question your industry is currently shouting about.

Right now there is a loud argument over whether "vibe-coding" — non-programmers building software by directing AI — is a revolution or a toy that produces demos and slop. I am a data point in that argument that anyone can download: a single person with no training, no funding, no team, no degree, and a diagnosed attention disorder, holding a working product in a month. It started with conversations, not code. My girlfriend and I would talk — really talk — about things like Ramanujan's scandalous equation (the one that says 1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + … somehow equals −1/12), or about the physicist David Bohm, whose life Oppenheimer helped ruin. But those conversations were so much fun that I had an idea: what if there were a romantic activity for couples built out of the most beautiful ideas humans ever had? Then, for our first anniversary, I made her a surprise: I vibe-coded a little 3D world where she could fly — she's a gamer, so she got a real flight joystick — over the places of her dreams, the Eiffel Tower, a Japanese shrine, while a tiny plane skywrote love messages behind the monuments. She stays out of this story by her own choice — she is a very private person, and I ask that she remain so — but she is the reason every game is built for two players on one screen, sharing one keyboard, forced to talk to each other. She loves me, not the games — and that sentence, I think, explains the project better than any press release could.
