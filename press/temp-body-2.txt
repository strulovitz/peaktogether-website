I'll give you the whole story in one paragraph, and then I'll unpack it. For a decade I was an anti-AI activist — I wrote a protest book and recorded protest songs on my electric guitar, warning this technology could be the end of us. And yet, in one month, I directed AI to build my life's work: a free, open-source "Disneyland of mathematics" — a virtual theme park where loving remakes of the classic '90s games Descent, Quake, Loom, and Homeworld secretly teach real university mathematics, two players on one screen, no signup, no ads, nothing for sale, ever. I built it because of the woman I love. Every sentence in this paragraph is true, every one is verifiable in one click, and I am a nobody from Israel with no PR agency — I'm sending this letter to many newsrooms at once, and whoever publishes first is simply first.

Here is where it really begins: on a couch, with a video playing. My girlfriend is not a scientist — math frightens her, like most normal people. But one evening I showed her Ramanujan's scandalous equation: 1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + … and instead of infinity, mathematics somehow answers −1/12 — something tiny, and negative, and a fraction. She didn't run. She got curious. Another evening, the Oppenheimer movie came up, and I confessed I refuse to watch it, because Oppenheimer helped ruin the life of one of my heroes: David Bohm — the physicist Einstein called his spiritual son, who in 1952 proposed a beautifully sane explanation for the central weirdness of quantum mechanics, and was given a cold shoulder for decades. She was curious about him too, so we watched Veritasium's video "Is This What Quantum Mechanics Looks Like?" — a droplet of silicone oil, bouncing on a vibrating bath, each bounce making ripples, each ripple steering the next bounce — a thing you can see, behaving like the deepest mystery in physics. And she — who remembered wave-particle duality vaguely from high school — simply got it. We sat there discussing the highest mathematics and physics, and enjoying it, together. A whole world that until that moment was alien to her suddenly became touchable. And I thought: this — THIS — should be a thing couples can do. So I decided to build her a world.

For our first anniversary, I made her a surprise: I used AI to build a little 3D world where she could fly — she's a gamer, so she got a real flight joystick — over the places of her dreams, the Eiffel Tower, a Japanese shrine, while a tiny plane skywrote love messages behind the monuments. She loved it. So I took the joystick code from her gift and connected it to the mathematics, and games started being born, one at a time. And when I proudly showed her the early website — all mountains and mathematics — she was honest with me, the way she always is: "I don't understand it. Who is this for? What do they do?" She was right. I rebuilt everything games-first because of that sentence. She stays out of this story by her own choice — she is a very private person, and I ask that she remain so — but her fingerprints are on every design decision, above all this one: every game is built for two players on one couch, sharing one keyboard, forced to talk to each other. She loves me, not the games — and that sentence, I think, explains the project better than any press release could. Now let me show you what her honesty produced.

In one game, two players fly a spaceship through the tunnels of an asteroid mine where the locked doors are the actual steps of a 300-year-old mathematical proof — and the weapons are named Euler, Newton, and Riemann: to open a door, you fire the mathematician whose idea it was. In another, Newton's Principia — the book that invented physics — has been rebuilt as a Quake dungeon from the original 1687 figures; you "read" the walls by shooting them, and they bloom from black-and-white into color. In a third, you don't see the mathematics at all: you hear it — a landscape performed live by a 13-instrument orchestra, where couples find a mountain pass together by ear, because a peak sounds like one bright chord and a saddle sounds eerie and stretched. In a fourth, a fleet of spaceships IS a mathematical matrix, and commanding it is — without the player quite noticing — a university course. Each game page at peaktogether.me has a one-minute gameplay video that autoplays the moment you open it; you can judge everything I just said in about five minutes. But the site is only half the story. The other half is that, by every normal rule, the person who made it had no right to be able to.

I genuinely cannot code. Not a line. I can't follow the proofs in my own games. What I did was direct — I described, argued, tested, rejected, and demanded, through hundreds of rounds — and AI wrote every line of code and every line of mathematics. Which brings me to the twist I promised: why a man like me spent ten years fighting the very technology that made all this possible.

From 2014 — before it was fashionable — I warned publicly that self-improving machines could end humanity.
