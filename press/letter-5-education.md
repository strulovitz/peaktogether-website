Subject: Story: A free "science museum" where teenagers learn university math by playing '90s-style games — no signup, no ads, no student data, and any teacher can build the next exhibit without writing code

Dear journalist,

I'll give you the whole story in one paragraph, and then I'll unpack it. There is a free, open-source "science museum" where students learn real university mathematics — never dumbed down — by playing loving remakes of the classic '90s games Descent, Quake, Loom, and Homeworld, two players on one screen: no signup, no ads, no data collected, nothing for sale, ever. It contains a new reading interface I believe education has never seen — the same proof written at four depth levels at once, which you move through physically, in 3D. And any teacher can build the next exhibit from any Wikipedia page, without knowing how to code, using a recipe published openly on the site. I know the recipe works, because I built the entire museum with it in one month — and I can't write "Hello World," I have ADD, and every year of high school they wanted to throw me out. I directed AI to build all of it, as a gift for my girlfriend. Stranger still: before building it, I spent a decade as an anti-AI activist. Every sentence in this paragraph is true, every one is verifiable in one click, and I am a nobody from Israel with no PR agency — I'm sending this letter to many newsrooms at once, and whoever publishes first is simply first.

Start with the invention, because it's the part your readers can use tomorrow. In the Descent remake — where two players fly a spaceship through an asteroid mine whose locked doors are the actual steps of a 300-year-old proof — there is something called Understanding Mode. Lock onto a concept and its explanation stretches ahead of you like road signs in fog: the nearest sign is crisp, the next visible faintly through it. Roll the mouse wheel and you move a reading depth — not the ship — forward and back. The same proof step is written at four levels simultaneously: rigorous graduate statement, working undergraduate intuition, plain high-school words, and an applied version with actual numbers. Think "explain like I'm five" — but for grown-ups, and continuous. Zoom in and you literally penetrate into finer detail; pull back and you literally see the big picture, the forest instead of the trees. Today's interfaces handle depth by scrolling and collapsing sections on a flat page — but humans are creatures of three-dimensional space, and "closer means deeper" is something the body already knows. And the colors are honest too: each core ingredient of a proof gets a primary color, and a secondary color may appear only when two ingredients genuinely combine — green only ever means "yellow idea meets blue idea." Color as a live dependency map, not decoration. None of this is math-specific: it works for any knowledge that comes in layers — which is most knowledge.

The rest of the museum keeps that standard. Newton's Principia rebuilt as a Quake dungeon from the original 1687 figures — every wall a real geometric proof, faithful to the printed page, which you "read" by shooting until it blooms into color (a method that would work for any diagram-rich book, from Euclid to modern visual textbooks). A game where you hear multivariable calculus: a landscape performed by a 13-instrument orchestra synthesized from 89 real Philharmonia samples — a peak sounds like one bright chord, a saddle point sounds eerie and stretched. I call the system sonifiquation — sonic + equation — and I'd ask your readers to think of it the way they think of graphs: a child today reads a rising stock chart instinctively, but only because we all trained in that language for centuries; a new notation always sounds foreign at first — and this one may matter most for blind and visually impaired students, who currently get almost nothing from the most visual subject on Earth. And a space strategy game where the fleet IS a matrix and commanding it is a linear algebra course. Everywhere, the same three rules: nothing is dumbed down — real proofs, real figures; you cannot lose — no health bars, no game-over, wrong answers get a friendly explanation, because you're not being tested, you're being shown; and two players, one screen — one flies, one thinks, and talking to each other is the game, because explaining to the person beside you is the oldest learning technology there is. Each game page at peaktogether.me has a one-minute gameplay video that autoplays on arrival; five minutes verifies everything. But the exhibits are not the story I'm pitching you. The story is who gets to build the next one.

Your readers are living inside a frightening debate: will AI replace the teacher? This museum is built on the opposite answer, and I want to state it carefully because I think it's the healthiest model anyone has proposed: the AI writes; the teacher directs and judges. The site publishes its full recipe: pick any Wikipedia page, download the free template (a flyable 3D demo of the harmonic series — the museum's workhorse), paste the provided starter prompt into an AI, and direct — no coding, no advanced math required. The teacher's irreplaceable job is to pretend to be the pupil and ask: is this actually clear? The teacher is the judge. And the teacher chooses which scenes to build from the parts that genuinely excite them — one will build the jeep-crossing-the-desert puzzle, another the tower of stacking blocks, another the coupon-collector's game — and that honest, personal excitement is the single most contagious thing in any classroom. Monday's lesson becomes a flight the teacher built over the weekend. Nor does this stop at school: the same recipe serves anyone who must pass knowledge to others — a manager introducing a new concept to her team, an instructor teaching a new technology. The museum is designed to grow the way Wikipedia grew: one exhibit per contributor, with the contributor's name on it, forever. Now — why does this museum exist at all? Because of a girl.

My girlfriend and I would talk — really talk — about things like Ramanujan's scandalous equation (1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + … = −1/12) and the physicist David Bohm, watching Veritasium videos together on the couch; she isn't a scientist, and math frightens her, but those conversations were so much fun that I wanted to build a place where couples could have them forever. For our first anniversary I built her a little flyable 3D world — she's a gamer — and that gift's joystick code became the museum's first demo. When I showed her the early site, all mathematics and mountains, she told me honestly: "I don't understand it. Who is this for?" — and I rebuilt everything games-first because of that sentence. She stays anonymous by her own choice, and I ask that she remain so. She loves me, not the games — and that sentence, I think, explains the project better than any press release could. But here is the part your readers' students should hear.

I described, argued, tested, rejected, and demanded, through hundreds of rounds, and AI wrote every line of code and every line of mathematics; the full source and commit history are public on GitHub, so the receipts are total. The kid the system almost threw away built a school disguised as an arcade, in a month.
